# 03 — Serve biomechanics research base

Prepared 27 September 2026 for the virtual serve coach project (recreational players, primarily UTR 1–7, Australian launch). This document is the evidence base for the coaching framework. It covers the phase model, reference values, recreational faults, injury epidemiology and referral red flags. It also sets out what a single phone camera can and cannot see.

**Evidence tags used throughout:** [Established] = peer-reviewed finding or strong consensus; [Coaching interpretation] = expert practice, not directly proven; [Vendor claim] = a company says so, unverified; [Market data]; [Regulation]; [Our inference] = our reasoning from the sources. "UNVERIFIED" marks anything I could not confirm from a primary source.

**How current is this?** The core lab literature dates from 2003–2016, and most of it is still the reference point. The most current synthesis is a 2026 narrative review by Martin, Touzard, Fourel and Pluim [S15]. Newer primary studies from 2024–2026 are included where I found them [S27, S28, S31, S48, S58].

## Summary: what matters for the product

1. **The research base describes elite servers much better than recreational ones.** Most lab studies use small samples of expert, able-bodied males (2–32 participants per study in the 2024 meta-analysis [S31]). Beginners, females, left-handers and kick/slice serves are under-studied [S15, S32]. [Established] Almost no lab sample sits in the UTR 1–7 range. The exceptions are the beginner and intermediate groups in Girard et al. [S6, S7], ITN 5–7 juniors [S8], ITN 8 non-experts [S46] and "advanced" club-level men [S12, S14]. [Our inference, from the samples listed]
2. **Leg drive is a genuine performance factor at every level.** When knee motion was restricted with splints, ball speed fell by about 15–17% in beginners, intermediates and elites alike [S7]. Elite players also produced higher vertical forces than beginners (2.12 vs 1.68 × body weight) and made contact higher (149% vs 143% of standing height) [S6]. [Established]
3. **Poor leg drive and poor sequencing load the arm without adding speed.** Olympic players with less than 10° of front-knee flexion at maximal external rotation served at similar speed. However, they had higher shoulder internal-rotation torque and elbow varus torque [S3]. In 19 expert men followed for two seasons after motion capture (the authors classify the design as case-control, level of evidence 3), those injured had poorer trunk-to-hand energy flow (71% vs 88%) and served about 11 km/h slower [S10]. [Established, small samples]
4. **Contact height and toss repeatability are central to both speed and consistency.** Impact height is roughly 1.4–1.5 × standing height [S6, S7, S29] and correlates with serve speed (ρ ≈ 0.75) [S28]. Lower variability in impact height strengthens the link between strength and speed [S28]. [Established]
5. **No single body fault explains a missed serve.** In two groups of elite junior female players and one professional woman, serves into the net differed from good serves mainly in ball projection angle, with fine adjustments at the elbow and wrist. They did not differ by one body segment [S23]. [Established] For the product, outcome metrics such as net vs long misses and toss or contact consistency should sit beside technique observations. [Our inference]
6. **Some commonly coached "faults" matter less than coaches think.** Foot-up and foot-back stances give similar racket speed and shoulder loads [S9, S15]. An abbreviated backswing gives similar speed and loads, with only a trend towards higher anterior shoulder force [S3]. The "waiter's serve" is the reverse case. It raised shoulder, elbow and wrist loads in elite juniors with no speed benefit [S16]. [Established]
7. **Trained experts can rate key features reliably from 2D video.** Two expert high-performance coaches rated the serves of 300 elite players (150 male, 150 female, three age groups) from two camera angles on a 1–7 scale. Agreement (Krippendorff's α) was 0.70–1.00 for overall serve, speed, rhythm, trunk action, leg drive and back-injury likelihood. It was weaker for shoulder internal-rotation speed (0.69–0.72) [S33]. [Established] The rated players were elite, not recreational, so reliability on UTR 1–7 serves is untested. This makes a credible target for automated 2D assessment. [Our inference]
8. **Serve injuries cluster in the shoulder, elbow, lumbar spine and abdominal wall.** The 2026 review estimates the overall incidence of lateral tennis elbow at 35–51%; shoulder injury ranges from 3% to 36% of able-bodied players, and low back pain from 3% to 72% [S15]. [Established] One secondary summary says the elbow (20%) and shoulder (15%) are the regions recreational players injure most often (UNVERIFIED; primary not accessed) [S44]. The app must screen for red flags and refer to a professional, never diagnose. [Our inference, based on clinical guidance in S55, S56]

## 1. The evidence landscape and its limits

- **Scope of the literature.** A 2024 scoping review found that 95% of serve studies examined kinematics and kinetics, mostly of the flat serve. Only about 20% examined physiological factors. The main gaps were kick and slice serves, left-handers, females, and younger or beginner players [S32]. [Established]
- **Pooled reference values exist but are noisy.** A 2024 meta-analysis covered 27 studies with players aged about 9 to 34, from national to professional level. It pooled joint angles at the trophy position, the racket low point and impact. The authors caution that differing measurement tools, anatomical definitions and rotation sequences limit precision. Samples ranged from 2 to 32 participants [S31]. [Established]
- **Injury-link evidence is thin.** The 2026 review notes that only a minority of studies pair biomechanical analysis with prospective injury follow-up. Methods for calculating joint loads vary widely between labs [S15]. [Established]
- **Implication.** Reference ranges in this document come mostly from elite or high-performance players. For UTR 1–7 users, the product should present them as direction of travel, for example "better servers make contact higher". They should not be presented as targets to hit exactly. [Our inference]

## 2. The phase model: Kovacs & Ellenbecker (2011) 8-stage model

Kovacs and Ellenbecker adapted the 6-stage overhead-throwing model into a serve-specific model. It has **3 phases and 8 stages** [S1]. The model is a descriptive teaching and clinical framework, not a validated measurement instrument. [Established as a published framework; its use as a coaching checklist is Coaching interpretation]

| Phase | Stage | What happens (paraphrased) | Key checkpoints and values cited in the paper | Injury implication stated |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Preparation | 1. Start | Set-up and stance; highly individual | Minimal muscle demand; align the body to use ground forces | Low demand |
| Preparation | 2. Release | Ball leaves the tossing hand | Toss slightly lateral to overhead. Contact abduction of about 100° is described as optimal | Toss too close to the head ("12 o'clock") raises arm abduction and may contribute to subacromial impingement |
| Preparation | 3. Loading | Knees flex, pelvis and shoulders tilt back, energy is stored | Front-knee flexion **>15°** recommended. Foot-up gives greater vertical force and reach; foot-back gives a wider base and deeper squat | Spinal hyperextension, lateral flexion and rotation load the facets; spondylolysis risk in developing players |
| Preparation | 4. Cocking | Racket drops behind the back to maximal external rotation (MER) | MER **172° ± 12°**, elbow flexion **104° ± 12°**, abduction **101° ± 13°** (elite data). Elite players can lose **up to 10–15°** of shoulder internal and total rotation | Abduction angles of 83–101° and arm lag carry impingement risk. Posterior shoulder stretches recommended |
| Acceleration | 5. Acceleration | Legs and trunk drive; the arm internally rotates | Advanced servers are described as going from MER to contact in under 1/100 s. Front-knee extension velocity about 800 ± 400°/s. Legs and trunk supply **51–55%** of kinetic energy and force to the hand | Very high pectoralis and subscapularis activity. Trunk "corkscrew" loading |
| Acceleration | 6. Contact | Ball impact | Trunk about **48° above horizontal**. Contact abduction about 101°, with 110° ± 15° proposed as optimal. Elbow about 20°, front knee about 24° (Olympic data) | — |
| Follow-through | 7. Deceleration | The arm decelerates eccentrically; internal rotation and pronation continue | Distraction forces **0.5–0.75 × body weight**; trunk–arm deceleration up to about 300 N·m | The "most violent" phase for posterior cuff eccentric load |
| Follow-through | 8. Finish | Landing and balance | Foot-up produces larger horizontal braking forces at front-foot landing | Eccentric leg load; landing training recommended |

Sources: [S1]; the elite kinematic values originate from Fleisig et al. 2003 [S4]; the energy figures cite Kibler 1995 [S59] (primary not accessed).

**Notes on these values.**
- The "under 1/100 s" MER-to-contact figure is quoted as stated [S1]. I could not verify it against a primary timing study, so treat it as UNVERIFIED. Any figure of this order means the acceleration phase lasts at most a few frames even at 240 fps. [Our inference]
- Kibler's modelling claim is also cited by Kovacs and Ellenbecker. It states that a 20% loss of trunk kinetic energy would need a 34% faster arm or 70% more arm mass to compensate [S1, S59]. This is a theoretical modelling estimate from a 1995 clinical review, widely repeated but not an empirical finding; the primary was not accessed. Treat it as illustrative only (UNVERIFIED primary). [Coaching interpretation]

**Product mapping.** The brief's seven product phases map onto the Kovacs stages as follows: setup = Start; toss = Release; loading = Loading; upward drive and cocking = Cocking; acceleration and contact = Acceleration and Contact; deceleration and landing = Deceleration and Finish. We add recovery (return to balance) as a product-level phase. No biomechanical norms exist for recovery. [Our inference]

## 3. Kinetic chain, segment contributions and timing

- **Proximal-to-distal sequence.** Twenty Olympic players were filmed at Sydney 2000 with two 200 Hz cameras. Peak angular velocities occurred in this order: trunk tilt (280°/s), upper-torso rotation (870°/s), pelvis rotation (440°/s), elbow extension (1,510°/s), wrist flexion (1,950°/s), then shoulder internal rotation. Internal rotation reached 2,420°/s in men and 1,370°/s in women. Ball speed was 50.8 m/s in men and 41.5 m/s in women. Women reached peak upper-torso velocity earlier (0.075 s vs 0.058 s before impact) [S4]. [Established]
- **Contributions to racket speed at impact.** Upper-arm internal rotation contributed about 54% and hand (wrist) flexion about 31%. Horizontal flexion and abduction contributed about 13%, and shoulder linear velocity about 10%, at a mean racket-head speed of 31 m/s [S5] (values via a secondary summary; primary abstract not opened, and not found in Europe PMC during fact-check — these four figures are UNVERIFIED). Elliott's 2006 review rounds these to internal rotation about 40%, hand flexion about 30%, upper-arm horizontal flexion about 15%, shoulder about 10% and pronation about 5%. He stresses that pronation mainly orients the racket face and that these figures ignore leg drive [S2]. [Established]
- **Energy flow and injury.** Nineteen high-level men (ITN 1–4) were followed for two seasons, and 11 developed upper-limb overuse injuries: 6 shoulder, 5 elbow and 1 wrist. The injured group had poorer trunk-to-hand energy-flow quality (71.1% vs 88.1%). They also served more slowly (159.5 vs 170.9 km/h) and absorbed more energy at the shoulder and elbow. Energy-flow quality correlated with ball speed (r = 0.55) and inversely with several shoulder and elbow loads [S10]. [Established, small n]
- **Timing and injury.** In a related prospective group, injured players showed delayed peak trunk and pelvis rotations and less ideal shoulder timing. They also had lower ball speed and higher upper-limb loads [S11]. One further pattern was "hyperangulation", where the arm stays horizontally abducted for too long (about 4.8% of serve duration). It was linked to 13–29% higher shoulder forces and about 11.5 km/h less ball speed (as summarised in [S15]). [Established, small n]
- **Angular momentum.** In ten players, trunk angular momentum about the anteroposterior axis related to ball velocity across the later phases. Transfer followed a proximal-to-distal timing from the trunk to the upper arm, forearm and hand-racket [S13]. [Established]
- **Stretch-shortening cycle.** Elliott reports a lab finding that upper-arm internal-rotation speed was about 20% higher with no pause than after a 1.5 s pause. He concludes that only a short pause should occur at maximum knee flexion [S2]. [Established mechanism; application to a "trophy pause" is Coaching interpretation]
- **Take-home.** Performance and lower joint loading are compatible. Players with better sequencing serve faster *and* load the arm less [S10]. [Established]

## 4. Lower body: knee flexion, leg drive, foot-up vs foot-back

- **Restricting the knees costs speed at every level.** Thirty right-handed men (7 beginner, 10 intermediate, 13 elite per the paper's tables; its methods text says 15 elite, which does not sum to 30) served with splints fixing the knees at 10° [S7]. Ball speed fell as follows:
  - beginners: 107.2 → 89.1 km/h
  - intermediates: 148.8 → 126.6 km/h
  - elites: 169.4 → 144.6 km/h

  Impact location also dropped. The authors conclude that knee motion contributes significantly whatever the performance level [S7]. [Established]
- **Better servers push harder and make contact higher.** Across 32 players, elite servers had higher peak vertical force than beginners (2.12 vs 1.68 × body weight). They also made contact higher (149.2% vs 142.6% of standing height) and served faster (169.4 vs 107.2 km/h). Their leg-muscle EMG peaks came earlier, at 79% vs 94% of the concentric phase [S6]. [Established]
- **Knee flexion and upper-limb load.** Olympic players with about 7.6° front-knee flexion served as fast (about 162 km/h) as those with about 14.7° (abstract values; the paper's table gives group means of 5.8° vs 15.9°, split at 10° of knee flexion at MER, with 162.2 vs 162.9 km/h). However, they had higher normalised shoulder internal-rotation torque and elbow varus torque. The authors recommend that all players flex the knees in the backswing [S3]. [Established] One nuance: Reid et al. compared foot-up, foot-back and "minimal leg drive" in 12 high-performance men. Minimal leg drive reduced racket speed, but shoulder kinetics were similar [S9]. The injury benefit of leg drive is therefore plausible but not settled. [Established — conflicting]
- **Intermediate juniors.** Thirty-two ITN 5–7 juniors were measured with inertial sensors. The greater-knee-flexion group (about 74.7° vs 55.6°) had higher pre-impact racket velocity (p = 0.004) and faster knee extension (535 vs 405°/s). Impact height did not differ [S8]. The reported racket-velocity difference is 3.33, labelled km/h in the abstract. Group means of about 21–24 suggest the unit may actually be m/s (UNVERIFIED). [Established]
- **Timing matters more than peak force.** Thirty-six competitive players served from two force plates [S48]:
  - Racket-head speed did **not** correlate with peak or mean vertical force, or with rate of force development.
  - It did correlate with the timing between peak vertical and peak forward force (r ≈ −0.52).
  - Relative net vertical impulse was the best predictor of impact height (r = 0.77).

  [Established] Kovacs Academy reads this as support for coaching an explosive, synchronised up-and-forward drive rather than simply a deeper knee bend [S49, S51]. [Coaching interpretation]
- **Foot-up vs foot-back.** The main trade-offs are:
  - Foot-up gives greater vertical force and reach, and larger braking forces at landing [S1].
  - Foot-back gives a wider base and allows a deeper squat [S1].
  - Both give higher racket speed than minimal leg drive, with similar shoulder kinetics [S9].
  - In juniors, upper-limb loads were similar for both stances [S15].

  [Established] Kovacs and Ellenbecker summarise foot-up vs foot-back as roughly 54° vs 66° of front-knee motion, 59° vs 45° of rear-knee extension, and peak rear-knee extension velocity of 9.3 vs 7.2 rad/s [S1]. Kovacs and Ellenbecker cite these figures to Reid et al. 2008 [S9] (their reference 48; confirmed in fact-check). An ITF review by Martin discusses tactical fit, for example foot-up for height and forward momentum, but only the abstract and metadata were accessible [S57]. **Product stance: do not "correct" a player's stance choice.** [Our inference]
- **Pooled trophy-position values.** Front knee 64.5° ± 9.7° (11 studies), back knee 67.4° ± 16.4° (8 studies) and trunk inclination 25.0° ± 7.1° (6 studies) [S31]. [Established]
  - *Correction (gap study, 27 Sep 2026).* "Trunk inclination" here is side bend of the thorax **relative to the pelvis** in the frontal plane. It is pooled from 15 data sets whose authors used different names ("lateral flexion", "trunk tilt", "shoulder–pelvis alignment separation angle"). It is not a trunk angle measured against vertical, so a 2D mid-hip to mid-shoulder angle cannot be compared with it. See `gaps/side-view-geometry-contradictions.md` §4.

## 5. Toss, contact point and serve direction

- **Toss height above contact.** Values from three samples:
  - High-performance men (mean UTR about 10.7): zenith 347.7 ± 24.0 cm, impact 291.5 ± 9.9 cm, so the ball fell about **0.56 m** from zenith to contact. Ball release to impact took **0.97 ± 0.10 s** [S27].
  - Thirteen nationally ranked junior men (15.8 years, 181 cm): zenith 3.40 m, impact 2.68 m, differential **0.72 ± 0.19 m**. Successful and faulted serves had almost identical toss values [S28].
  - A study comparing a professional with a competitive player reported the competitive player's toss about 39 cm higher (secondary summary; UNVERIFIED).

  [Established] Physics check: a 0.56–0.72 m fall takes 0.34–0.38 s, so the ball "hangs" for about a third of a second before contact. [Our inference]
- **Contact height relative to body height.** Reported values:
  - Elite 149% vs beginners 143% [S6].
  - 144–149% across levels in a later study, which cites 141–152% from earlier work [S7].
  - A roughly constant 1:1.5 ratio in experienced players, even under artificial crosswind [S29].
  - About 148% for the juniors in [S28] (268/181 cm, our calculation).

  Impact height correlated with serve speed (ρ = 0.75–0.78), and still did after normalising for height (ρ = 0.63) [S28]. [Established]
- **Consistency is regulated around the ball.** Experienced servers stabilise the vertical toss dimension and allow more side-to-side and forward-back variation [S29]. Elite female servers show a variable toss but very consistent serve timing. This suggests they use the ball's location to regulate the movement [S24]. More movement variability, and less predictable variability, went with lower speed and accuracy in 17 players [S30]. [Established]
- **Disguise and targeting.** Six professionals changed front-foot position, toss zenith and impact location between T, body and wide first serves. Their second serves were kinematically consistent across targets [S20]. [Established]
- **Deuce vs ad court.** In 14 elite junior males (300 Hz Vicon), impact was further to the left on the deuce court (30.0 vs 10.3 cm). Front-knee flexion at impact (29.1° vs 26.2°) and upper-torso range of motion were also greater on the deuce court, while serve speed was similar (about 151 km/h) [S34]. [Established] **Reference values must be stored per court side.** [Our inference]
- **Tossing arm.** In 10 high-performance men, telling players to drop the tossing arm earlier or later changed the ball trajectory by about 1–3° and raised the toss. It left racket speed unchanged (about 157 km/h) but wrecked accuracy: 87% on target normally, vs 17% (early) and 10% (late). Head kinematics were unaffected [S27]. [Established] In 14 professionals, a qualitative analysis found tossing-arm and toss position at ball release shifted as match time and dehydration increased (r ≈ −0.73 to −0.74 with match time) [S47]. [Established, small n, qualitative]
- **Practising the toss in isolation changes the toss.** Five elite juniors tossed higher when tossing alone than when serving, and the racket–ball timing coupling (r = 0.861 in full serves) was disrupted [S25]. [Established] **Toss-only drills should be used sparingly and followed by full serves.** [Our inference]
- **Aces are about placement as much as speed.** A total of 25,680 Australian Open first serves by 151 men were analysed using Hawk-Eye data. Direction relative to the returner, closeness of the landing to a service-box line, and speed together classified aces with 87% accuracy [S21]. [Established] (Tennis Australia sport science.)

## 6. Upper body: trunk tilt, racket drop, internal rotation, common arm patterns

- **Shoulder-over-shoulder.** Elite servers tilt the trunk so the hitting shoulder rises over the tossing shoulder rather than rotating horizontally. This positions upper-arm internal rotation to do its work [S2]. At impact the trunk is about 48° above horizontal [S4]. *Correction (gap study, 27 Sep 2026):* the methods definition of this 48° could not be retrieved. It is most plausibly the shoulder line's elevation above horizontal, i.e. shoulder-over-shoulder tilt, but a 2024 meta-analysis files it under trunk flexion [S31]. Treat it as an unresolved 3D angle. It is not a target for any 2D angle (`gaps/side-view-geometry-contradictions.md` §4). In elite females, leg drive and shoulder-over-shoulder rotation matured *later* in development than the serving arm's contribution [S22]. [Established]
- **Racket drop and MER.** MER is 172° in Olympic players [S4]. The pooled shoulder external rotation at the racket low point is 130° ± 26.5° [S31]; definitions differ, so values vary. MER cannot be measured from 2D video, because true shoulder rotation needs 3D. [Our inference] A deep, relaxed racket drop with the racket pointing down behind the back is a Coaching interpretation proxy [S53].
- **Waiter's serve.** This is the palm-up, open-face racket position during cocking. Eighteen French top-10 juniors aged 12–15 were compared [S16]:
  - The waiter's group had higher shoulder internal-rotation torque, elbow varus torque and wrist forces.
  - Serve speed was the same.
  - They also had lower shoulder abduction and more wrist extension during cocking.
  - The 12-month injury difference was not significant.

  [Established] Extending this to adult recreational players is [Our inference].
- **Abbreviated backswing.** Similar speed and upper-limb kinetics, with a trend (p = 0.05) towards higher anterior shoulder force [S3]. [Established] **Low priority to change.** [Our inference]
- **Skill level and efficiency.** Seven advanced players served at 143.3 km/h and 11 professionals at 177.8 km/h. The advanced players had higher shoulder and elbow loads per unit of ball speed [S12]. [Established] This is the closest lab evidence that club players pay a higher load cost for their speed. [Our inference]
- **Fatigue.** After a 3-hour match, 8 advanced players showed lower ball speed and impact height, and most upper-limb loads decreased. The timing of peak angular velocities was preserved [S14]. [Established]

## 7. Serve types: flat, slice, kick

- **Racket speed is similar across serve types; racket direction differs.** Seven NCAA Division I men were captured outdoors with markerless capture [S19]:
  - The kick had the largest sideways and smallest forward racket velocity.
  - The flat had the smallest upward component.
  - The slice combined sideways velocity with large forward velocity.
  - At kick contact the racket was 8.7 cm further back and 21.1 cm further towards the body midline, relative to the shoulder, than for the flat.

  [Established]
- **Contact position differs.** Elite adolescents made contact about 17 cm further forward on flat serves than kick serves [S35]. [Established]
- **Riskiest window.** An earlier review identified late cocking and early acceleration as the phases with the highest internal forces and injury risk [S17]. [Established]
- **Loads by serve type are unresolved.** Some studies report more force at the back and more backward-directed shoulder force for the kick [S18]. Others find greater lumbar flexion moments for the flat serve [S36], or similar shoulder loads [S15]. The 2026 review calls this an open question [S15]. [Established — conflicting]
- **Spin cannot be read off body kinematics.** Spin type and rate depend on racket-face angle and path at contact. That window lasts milliseconds and is largely set by pronation and internal rotation [S1]. [Established] A phone can at most *classify* the swing direction (sideways vs forward) and the ball's flight and bounce. It cannot measure spin rate. [Our inference]

[Established] **Age and sex.** Prepubescent girls have lower racket velocity. The serving arm's contribution grows after puberty, while leg drive and shoulder-over-shoulder rotation mature later [S22]. **Implication.** Rate leg drive and trunk tilt on a player-relative scale, adjusted for self-reported age, sex and physical comfort. Do not rate them against Olympic norms. [Our inference]

## 9. Phase table: checkpoints, reference ranges, and which camera sees what

**Camera views (our definitions).**
- **Side view (S):** phone on a tripod beside the court on the server's hitting-arm side, roughly level with the baseline, 4–6 m away, lens at about 1.0–1.2 m. At the trophy position it faces the server's chest, and the serve direction runs across the frame. It sees motion towards the court, vertical heights and shoulder-line tilt.
- **Rear view (R):** phone behind the server, in line with the serve direction, ideally raised to 1.5 m or more with the service box lines in frame. It sees the toss's left–right position, the racket's sideways path, knee bend in profile (the body faces the side fence at the trophy position), and ball flight and landing.

The view assignments are [Our inference] from serve geometry. Wood et al. used two viewing angles for reliable expert ratings, but the angles are not described in the abstract [S33].

*Correction (gap study, 27 Sep 2026; `gaps/side-view-geometry-contradictions.md`).*
- **Distance and height.** At 4–6 m and 1.0–1.2 m with a level phone, the side view cannot frame the toss: the frame top in the ball's plane is about 2.5–3.5 m on a 24 mm lens. The side view needs a frame top of at least 4.2 m (§6 of the gap study).
- **Knee assignment confirmed.** Measured front-foot angles (31–40° to the baseline) and shoulder turn (106–121° from square at maximum preparation) put the knees 50–90° out of the side-view plane at the trophy position, and 0–40° out of the rear-view plane. The rear-view knee assignment below is therefore right.
- **Trunk tilt at contact is a rear-view metric.** At contact the shoulder line is 65–84° out of the side-view plane, so the side view cannot see shoulder-over-shoulder tilt. Its forward-lean reading is also contaminated on the court half nearer the camera.

**Frame-rate arithmetic [Our inference].** At 30, 60, 120 and 240 fps, one frame lasts 33.3, 16.7, 8.3 and 4.2 ms respectively.
- A racket head at 31–47 m/s [S1, S5] moves about 1.0–1.6 m per frame at 30 fps, but only 0.13–0.20 m at 240 fps.
- Near contact the falling ball travels about 3.3 m/s, so it moves about 5.5 cm per frame at 60 fps and about 1.4 cm per frame at 240 fps. Contact height is therefore quantised to roughly one of those steps before any curve fitting (about ±2.8 cm and ±0.7 cm if the nearest frame is chosen).
- The toss zenith is easy to capture even at 30 fps, because the ball is nearly stationary there.
- The whole release-to-impact window (about 1 s [S27]) is 30 frames at 30 fps. That is enough for toss metrics but not for the acceleration phase.

**Priority logic for UTR 1–7 [Our inference].**
- Goal = more first serves in: target toss repeatability and contact height first.
- Goal = more pace: target leg-drive timing and trunk-before-arm sequencing, then grip.

## 11. Serve-related injury epidemiology

- **Overall incidence.** A 2025 systematic review of 37 studies (16 adolescent, 18 adult, 3 mixed) reported 2.11–3.50 injuries per 1,000 player-hours in juniors and 1.25–56.6 in adults; the upper figure comes from tournament settings. Lower-limb injuries predominate (48–56%; ankle sprains 18–25% in juniors and 15–22% in adults). The lumbar spine accounts for 24–39% of junior injuries; in adults the review reports the *trunk* (not the lumbar spine specifically) at 12–24%. Shoulder tendinopathy accounts for 25–40% of adult injuries [S43]. [Established] Note: the review included only high-performance players (academy/national juniors and professional adults) and explicitly excluded recreational and amateur studies, so these proportions may not transfer to UTR 1–7 adults. [Our inference]
- **Serve share.** The serve accounted for 10.3–12.0% of injuries in NCAA data, vs 6.4–8.0% for the forehand and 3.0–5.2% for the backhand (as summarised in [S15]). [Established]
- **Shoulder** (rotator cuff tendinopathy, labral/SLAP lesions, internal impingement, instability). Prevalence is 3–36% in able-bodied players [S15]:
  - Elite players can lose up to 10–15° of dominant-shoulder internal and total rotation [S1].
  - Among 100 professionals, internal-rotation deficits correlated with shoulder pain [S40].
  - Among 47 professionals, limited internal-rotation range (rather than side-to-side difference) went with a history of shoulder pain [S41].
  - Among 58 elite players, external-rotation weakness went with a history of shoulder pain [S42].

  [Established]
- **Elbow** (lateral epicondylalgia, medial or flexor-pronator problems). The overall incidence of lateral tennis elbow is estimated at 35–51% [S15]. An epidemiological study of 532 players aged 20–50 found incidence and recurrence rising with age and playing time [S45] (abstract confirmed via Europe PMC during fact-check: 278 men, 254 women). Recreational players reportedly injure the elbow (20%) and shoulder (15%) most often [S44] (secondary summary; UNVERIFIED primary). Waiter's serve and low leg drive raise elbow varus load [S16, S3]. [Established]
- **Lumbar spine.** Findings in elite juniors:
  - 28 of 33 asymptomatic elite adolescents (84.8%) had MRI abnormalities, and 9 had pars lesions, mostly at L5 [S38].
  - Serve lateral-flexion forces are about eight times those of running, and are higher in adolescents with past disabling low back pain [S36].
  - Those players also show reduced lower-lumbar mobility and altered timing [S37].
  - In exploratory timing data, juniors with lumbar abnormalities tended to reach peak front-knee flexion and start pelvis rotation earlier; the authors did not test this statistically [S35]. (Tentative, not Established.)

  [Established] Adult recreational data are sparse. [Our inference]
- **Abdominal wall.** Strain of the non-dominant rectus abdominis during the cocking phase is the accepted mechanism [S39]. [Established]
- **Knee and lower limb.** Lower-limb injuries dominate tennis overall [S43], but I found no strong serve-specific knee-injury evidence. Foot-up landings bring larger braking forces [S1]. [Established; serve-knee link is a gap]
- **Workload.** The 2025 review reports that an acute:chronic workload ratio above 1.3 in juniors raised injury risk about 1.6-fold, and above 1.5 in adults gave a hazard ratio of about 2.8 for new injury. It also reports prevention effects: a four-week ramp-up cut total injuries by 21% in professionals, core-stability training cut overuse injuries by 26%, and grip-size fitting reduced lateral elbow pain from 14% to 6% [S43]. These figures were confirmed in the review during fact-check; the underlying primary studies were not checked, and all come from high-performance players. [Established, via systematic review]

## 13. Expert coaching sources and how they relate to the research

**Dr Mark Kovacs (Kovacs Institute / Kovacs Academy).**
- Co-author of the 8-stage model [S1]. He runs a Tennis Serve Specialist certification built on that model [S49].
- Sells a remote "Tennis Serve Screen" at $499, or $999 with a 4-week programme [Market data]. The prices are assumed to be USD, about A$710 and A$1,421 at 1 USD = 1.4224 AUD (ECB rate, 25 Sep 2026) [S52, S60].
- Priority faults he names for competitive adults: insufficient leg drive (focus on vertical back-hip displacement rather than depth), weak core and oblique engagement, and poor shoulder alignment. The Kovacs Academy summary also lists "increasing pronation" and a relaxed grip [S51]. [Coaching interpretation] Clarified (addendum, `gaps/intuitive-tennis-kovacs-primary-content.md` §4.3): in the talk itself Kovacs frames this as the speed of "long-axis rotation" (shoulder internal rotation plus pronation), not conscious pronation, and elsewhere he says pronation should never be taught directly. He puts the speed of the back hip's vertical rise, not knee-bend depth, first among speed correlates. He also gives about 60% (sometimes 70%) back-leg weight at the lowest hip point and a 10–15° hip turn in loading. These lab claims are unverified.
- Recommends single-leg (back-leg) serving as his "most important" drill. He states that good servers lose only 5–7 mph (8–11 km/h) serving off the back leg alone [S50]. [Vendor claim]

**Intuitive Tennis (Nikola Aracic).**
- Sells a 41-lesson, 2-hour "Intuitive Serve" course for US$299 (about A$425) [S53, S60] [Market data].
- Philosophy: build fundamentals and acceleration so that technical elements happen without conscious thought. The course includes a "serve myths" chapter covering pronation, leg drive, the throwing-motion analogy, wrist snap and "hitting up" [S53]. [Coaching interpretation]
- A video titled "The Passive Leg Drive" advises players not to actively jump [S54]. [Coaching interpretation] Corrected (addendum §2.3): the content is now verified from the video's captions and two related lessons. Lift-off should be a passive result of the trunk straightening out of a "reverse C" and the shoulder "cartwheel". Some knee bend and getting onto the toes are still required. Players should not try to load the back foot at the trophy, because a toss in front puts most of the weight on the front foot.

**Where these sources meet the research [Our inference].**
- Both schools agree the legs matter. They differ on cueing: Kovacs uses active loading and explosive transition, while Intuitive Tennis uses a passive, relaxed drive. Both reject "bend deeper" and pausing at the bottom. They also disagree on where the weight sits at the trophy (Kovacs: mostly on the back leg at the lowest hip point; Aracic: mostly on the front foot) and on toss height (addendum §5).
- The evidence supports "some flexion plus good timing" [S3, S7, S48]. It does not support "deeper is always better".
- The research on disrupting the tossing arm [S27] supports Intuitive Tennis's caution against over-mechanising single elements.
- The research on task decomposition [S25] questions heavy use of part-practice. The knee-serve drill gets only partial support [S26].
- In recreational youth, practice spacing made little difference when practice volume was controlled [S58].

**ITF / Tennis Australia / UWA.**
- Elliott, Reid and Crespo's ITF book *Biomechanics of Advanced Tennis* (2003) underpins much of this work [S62]. It was not accessed; cited via [S2].
- Tennis Australia sport science (Reid, Whiteside and colleagues) contributed the work on the toss, targeting, ace prediction, age effects, junior lumbar loading and 2D expert reliability [S20–S27, S33, S35–S37].

## Implications for the product

1. **Build the first-version analysis around what 2D video supports well.**
   - Toss zenith height above contact, toss forward and lateral position, and toss consistency.
   - Contact height as a percentage of the player's entered height.
   - Release-to-contact timing and landing position.
   - Coarse ratings (low / medium / high) of leg drive, trunk tilt and rhythm. These mirror the features experts rate reliably [S33].
2. **Do not report joint angles as precise numbers.** The exceptions are an "approximate knee bend" band from the rear or oblique view, and trunk tilt from the side view, both labelled "estimate ± range". *Correction (gap study, 27 Sep 2026): the side-view trunk band should be shoulder tilt at the trophy position; shoulder-over-shoulder at contact needs the rear view.* Never show MER, internal-rotation velocity, pronation or racket-face angle [S31, S33]. [Our inference]
3. **Recommend the side view as the single default and the rear view as the second angle.**
   - The side view carries contact height, forward contact, trunk tilt and landing. *Correction (27 Sep 2026): it also carries leg drive (hip rise); its trunk tilt means shoulder tilt at the trophy position only.*
   - The rear view unlocks toss left–right position, the sideways racket path (a serve-type hint), knee bend in profile, ball landing and serve-in rate.
   - Require 120 fps or more for any contact-frame metric; 240 fps is preferred. Allow 60 fps for toss-only metrics (Section 9 arithmetic). [Our inference]
4. **Collect player height at onboarding.** It is the scale factor for contact-height ratio and toss metrics. Also ask for stance preference, age band, sex (optional) and pain history. [Our inference]
5. **Coaching decision tree, in priority order.**
   - (1) Pain or red flag → refer (Section 12).
   - (2) First-serve % goal → toss consistency or contact height.
   - (3) Pace goal → leg-drive timing and trunk-before-arm sequencing.
   - (4) Second-serve reliability → grip plus a sideways/upward swing path.

   Suppress low-value "fixes": stance type and abbreviated backswing [S3, S9]. [Our inference]
6. **Label every insight with its evidence tier in the UI**, for example "Research-backed", "Coach-recommended" or "Experimental". Show the population behind each reference range, for example "elite adult men". [Our inference]
7. **Favour whole-serve drills over isolated toss practice** [S25]. Cues should target outcomes (the ball's position and height), because player-directed changes to single elements can wreck accuracy [S27]. [Our inference]
8. **Build in load safety.**
   - Session serve counts and a weekly ramp warning [S43].
   - Fatigue flags when contact height or speed proxies drop within a session [S14].
   - Extra caution for juniors (lumbar spine) and for kick-serve volume [S36, S38].
9. **Validation plan hooks** (for the accuracy workstream).
   - Compare app toss and contact heights with manually digitised 240 fps video, and with the literature ranges above.
   - Compare coarse ratings with two blinded coaches, using Krippendorff's α with a target of at least 0.7, matching [S33].
   - A chest-strap inertial sensor showed high repeat-measure reliability for torso and toss-related parameters in amateur players [S61]. This offers a possible later "second sensor" option (Apple Watch or strap). [Established] / [Our inference]
10. **Commercial benchmark.** A self-paced expert video course costs about A$425 (Intuitive Serve, US$299, no personal analysis) [S53], and a personal expert serve video analysis costs about A$710, or A$1,421 with a monitored 4-week programme (Kovacs Serve Screen, US$499/US$999) [S52, S60]. [Market data] An app offering a credible, repeatable version of personal analysis has price headroom, although these are one-off purchases, not subscriptions. [Our inference]

## Open questions

1. What are typical toss, contact-height and knee-flexion values for UTR 1–7 adults, especially women and players over 40? No lab dataset exists, so we will need to build our own reference set.
2. How accurate is phone-based 2D pose estimation for knee flexion and trunk tilt during the serve, given foreshortening from oblique knee planes? We need our own validation against 3D or manual digitising.
3. Does coaching leg drive in recreational adults reduce shoulder or elbow symptoms? The evidence conflicts [S3, S9] and has no recreational data.
4. Is the kick serve riskier for older recreational backs and shoulders? Loads by serve type are unresolved [S15].
5. Which single cue produces the largest first-serve % gain in 1–2 sessions for UTR 1–7 players? No intervention trials were found.
6. Can a rear-view phone reliably classify serve type (flat/slice/kick) from the racket path and ball flight? This is not tested in the literature.
7. What is the exact MER-to-contact duration (the "under 1/100 s" figure in [S1] is UNVERIFIED), and does a 120 fps capture reliably find the contact frame on recreational serves?
8. I could not open the full texts of Wood et al. 2023 [S33] (camera angles), Elliott et al. 1995 [S5], Jayanthi et al. 2005 [S44], Kibler 1995 [S59] or the ITF books [S62]. These should be obtained before claims are finalised in-product.
