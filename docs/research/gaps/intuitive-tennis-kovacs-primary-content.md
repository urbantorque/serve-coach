# Addendum to 04: primary teaching of Intuitive Tennis (Nikola Aracic) and Dr Mark Kovacs

Prepared 27 September 2026 for the virtual serve coach (iOS, Australian launch, UTR 1–7). This addendum closes the gap `intuitive-tennis-kovacs-primary-content`. It checks what the two named expert sources actually teach, using free public material only. It then audits every Intuitive Tennis attribution in the research 04 §6 fault library (F1–F18), benchmarks the Kovacs Serve Screen against D1 and research 06, sets D6 defaults where the experts disagree, and advises how the app can refer to these coaches without implying endorsement.

**Evidence labels (as in research 04):** [Established] peer-reviewed or strong consensus; [Coaching interpretation] expert practice, not directly proven; [Vendor claim] a coach or company says so about itself or its results; [Regulation]; [Our inference]. "UNVERIFIED" marks anything we could not confirm from a primary source. Sources in this file are numbered **A1–A75**. Sources in the other research files are cited as (research 04 [S12]).

**How the material was read (method and limits).**
- **Intuitive Tennis.** We reviewed 45 free YouTube lessons and interviews. For each, we read the video page, the description and the caption text (manual English captions where they exist, otherwise YouTube's auto-generated captions). Timestamps (m:ss) are the start of the caption segment and are accurate to within a few seconds. Auto-captions mangle names, so we paraphrase and never rely on a single mis-heard word. No video or audio was downloaded.
- **Paywalled Intuitive Tennis pages.** The "Problems at the Rec Level" videos on intuitivetennis.com show only a title and a one-line description, for example the Double Knee Bend page (research 04 [S9]). Their teaching is still unseen. Where a free YouTube lesson covers the same topic, we use that and say so.
- **Blocked pages.** The "What is the Intuitive Zone?" page (intuitivetennis.clickfunnels.com) returned a Cloudflare bot challenge, and we did not bypass it. No free podcast or interview in which Aracic discusses serve technique was found other than those listed (A41); two other podcast transcripts we checked (Tennisnerd 2023 and 2024, EMAzing 2022) were mostly about racquets and the tours.
- **Kovacs.** We re-read the Kovacs Academy posts cited in research 04 and 03. Several newer posts are members-only (A68). We also read the captions of Kovacs's public talks and podcast clips (A46–A56). The *Strength and Conditioning Journal* full text is paywalled (HTTP 402/403), so that paper is verified at bibliographic and abstract level only (§4.2).
- **Compliance note [Our inference].** Caption text was retrieved as plain text by a small script that calls YouTube's public player interface. It was used for reading only. YouTube's Terms restrict automated access and reproduction of content (A44). This method should not be repeated at scale or built into the product; future reviews should read transcripts in YouTube's on-page transcript panel. Nothing in this file reproduces transcript text beyond short paraphrase.

## 1. Key takeaways

1. **Intuitive Tennis's teaching is now verified from free lessons on every topic in the brief except the "1-2-3 rhythm" and the literal content of "control before power".** Both remain paywalled lesson titles. The "1-2-3 rhythm" drill that research 04 F4 and F10 attribute to Intuitive Tennis has **no verified content**, so we removed that attribution (§3). [Coaching interpretation for the verified content]
2. **Several research 04 attributions were wrong or overstated.** Research 04 F12 cites Intuitive Tennis for the cue "Be tall", but the course lists "be tall" among its serve **myths**. F10's cause ("a conscious 'bend your knees' cue") is not Aracic's explanation. F1's video symptom would flag Federer-style open-face racquet drops as a waiter's tray, which Aracic explicitly says they are not (A21). The two Kovacs Academy posts that research 04 attributes to Kovacs (80/20 and style vs fundamentals) were written by a Ki Serve Fellow and the Ki team, reporting Kovacs's observations (A57, A58). All of these are corrected in 04 and listed in §3 and the verification log.
3. **The two schools agree more than research 03/04 implied.** Both say pronation must never be consciously taught (A22, A51 39:09). Both say "bend deeper" is the wrong goal: timing and the speed of coming out of the bend matter more (A10, A46 9:00). Both put the toss in front: Aracic says always inside the baseline (A6), and Kovacs & Ellenbecker say slightly in front and to the hitting side (research 04 [S12]). Both treat landing inside the court as a *result*, not something to coach directly (A11 5:50; A46 16:45). Both reject waiting: Aracic at the trophy (A20), Kovacs at the bottom of the load or for a falling ball (A47 2:18; A46 32:30). And both use a straight tossing arm with a release at about eye level (A1; A46 26:25). [Coaching interpretation]
4. **They genuinely disagree on four things that matter for D6 defaults.** These are: whether leg drive is an active push (Kovacs) or a passive result of trunk and shoulder action (Aracic); where the weight should be at the trophy position (Kovacs: about 60–70% on the back leg at the lowest hip point; Aracic: mostly on the front foot once the toss is in front); toss height (Aracic: higher is safer for most recreational players; Kovacs: height is style, err lower, and shorten everything for players with little practice time); and whether the serve is a throwing motion. See §5. [Coaching interpretation]
5. **Kovacs gives more usable adult values than research 04 recorded.** For release height he gives a window between the eyes and the top of the head, less than about 15 cm wide (A46 26:25; A54 0:56). The tossing arm travels towards 1:30–2:30 on a clock face, roughly towards the right net post for a right-hander (A46 28:49; A51 21:32). He puts the toss apex at no more than about 1.5 ft (≈46 cm) above full racquet reach (A46 32:30). He suggests a 10–15° back-hip turn during loading (A46 14:13; A47 5:55), and landing about 1–2 ft (≈30–60 cm) inside the baseline (A46 15:56). The two strongest correlates of serve speed in his account are the speed of the back hip's vertical displacement and the speed of "long-axis rotation" (shoulder internal rotation plus pronation) (A46 5:33–8:08). [Coaching interpretation; the lab claims behind these are UNVERIFIED]
6. **The Kovacs Serve Screen's filming specification is not public.** The specification is given only after registration (A63). Public Kovacs Institute advice is simply to film from behind and from the side (A57). His two "80/20" stages, release and loading, include checkpoints that D1's single side view cannot see: tossing-arm direction, hip turn and hip/shoulder tilt. The optional rear view therefore has a *mechanics* role, not only an outcomes role (§4.1). [Our inference]
7. **Kovacs's toss-height upper bound (≈46 cm above reach) is lower than the elite values in research 03 (≈0.56–0.72 m drop).** Aracic, for his part, prefers a higher toss. The app should therefore not prescribe a toss height. It should measure the player's own apex-to-contact drop and its consistency (D5), and treat *waiting* (a trophy pause or stalled legs) as the fault, not height itself (§5). [Our inference]
8. **Referring to these coaches.** Crediting a coach with teaching they did not give risks a false or misleading representation of sponsorship, approval or affiliation under ACL s 29(1)(g)–(h). Since 28 March 2026 the maximum penalty for a company is the greater of A$100 million, three times the benefit, or 30% of adjusted turnover (A69, A76). The platform terms of both sources also prohibit commercial reuse of their content (A43, A44). Paraphrase ideas, cite neutrally, never use names in cues, features or App Store metadata, and state independence (§6). [Regulation; applying it to our facts is Our inference and needs legal review]

## 2. Intuitive Tennis: what is actually taught (free material)

### 2.1 Philosophy and the "intuitive zone"

| Claim taught | Source (timestamp) | Label |
|---|---|---|
| Beginners' instincts are "fundamentally wrong": they push the strings at the ball with a shallow toss because that feels safest. Players become intuitive only after learning fundamentals and doing many repetitions. | A30 0:17–1:26; A5 0:34–1:45 | [Coaching interpretation] |
| **Intuitive zone:** once fundamentals and acceleration are both present, fine technical details develop by themselves in the fastest part of a stroke (from shortly before to shortly after contact). This window lasts milliseconds and is invisible in real time. | A31 0:00–0:38; A30 2:05–4:43 | [Coaching interpretation] |
| Trying to adjust anything consciously inside that window forces the player to shorten and slow the stroke. Examples he gives are pronation, racquet drop and tossing-arm tuck. | A30 4:57–5:27; A22 0:22–1:32; A21 5:06–6:02 | [Coaching interpretation] |
| Style (stance, take-back, toss-arm path, trophy variant, finish) should suit the player. Fundamentals are what all high-level servers share: a deep racquet drop, pronation into contact, contact position by serve type, and a slightly lateral delivery. | A28 0:00–9:30, 9:30–13:53 | [Coaching interpretation] |

**Fit with research [Our inference].** The idea is compatible with the implicit-learning and external-focus literature (research 04 §5), and with the finding that instructed changes to one element, such as tossing-arm timing, damaged accuracy in high-performance players (research 03 [S27]). Aracic does not cite research. His observations about professionals (for example that about 95% do not always "continue to pronate") are his own frame-by-frame review, and he says he cannot show the evidence (A23 1:30–3:04). [Vendor claim]

### 2.2 The toss: release, placement, height, and "why the toss is difficult"

| Topic | What is taught (paraphrase) | Source (timestamp) |
|---|---|---|
| Why it matters | The toss is "the most important technical aspect" of the serve and the biggest recreational serve problem. A bad toss forces improvised corrections. Even elite players struggle with it. | A1 0:00; A8 0:00; A3 0:07 |
| **Why the toss is difficult** | (1) The two arms do different jobs at once: the non-dominant arm tosses while the hitting arm takes the racquet back. He compares it to a non-drummer playing two rhythms. (2) Most people never throw with the non-dominant hand. (3) The toss arm speeds up near the top, so players cannot perceive their release point, and there is a delay between intending to let go and letting go. (4) A dry toss is easy, but it breaks down once rhythm and weight transfer are added. The commonest error at low recreational levels is poor synchronisation of the arms. Advanced recreational players show finer errors in release, tempo, arm structure and hand position. | A41 53:11–55:09; A32 0:00–0:26; A2 0:00–0:47; A6 7:18–7:54; A8 0:00–0:31 |
| Release height | "Release at eye level" is a problematic cue: because of the delay, players let go too late (forehead or higher) and the ball drifts back. Instead, *intend* to release between chin and nose, which usually yields an actual release at eye level. An older lesson suggests intending between forehead and chin. | A2 0:00–1:23; A3 2:51–3:15; A32 0:49–1:10 |
| Arm and hand | A straight arm is optimal (not hyperextended). A consistently slightly bent arm can work; changing the arm's shape during the toss does not. Hold the ball on the base of the fingers, slightly in the palm. Release it "flat" by spreading the fingers so the ball barely spins. Tempo goes slow to fast. | A1 0:55–2:03, 4:00–5:25; A3 1:59–2:51, 3:26–7:04 |
| Toss-arm path | Three styles: parallel to the baseline (disguise and automatic coil, but hard to get into the court: not recommended for recreational players), diagonal along the front foot towards the right net post (the most common style, and recommended) and straight forward (hard to toss behind, poor disguise). With parallel or diagonal tosses the ball must arc like a basketball "skyhook" and drop through the target, not drift further left. | A7 0:00–5:46; A3 7:36–9:14; A8 1:40–2:18 |
| Placement reference | Judge "in front" and "behind" against the **baseline**, not the body, because the body moves during the serve. Never toss behind the baseline on any serve, including kick. Not too far in front either, because the player then opens the face and misses long. | A6 0:51–2:54, 6:02–6:42 |
| Placement by serve type | On a clock with 12 o'clock at the front (non-dominant) shoulder: flat 12–1 o'clock, and avoid 11. For slice, intermediates may toss to 2 o'clock for an "automatic" slice (at some cost in power), while advanced players should learn slice from 12. Kick must be at least 12 o'clock, or 11:00–11:30 if flexible, and only just inside the baseline. Hitting all three serves from 12 is ideal for disguise. | A6 7:54–11:49, 13:54–14:35; A33 7:28–9:55; A34 0:10–0:44 |
| Height | It is always better to toss too high than too low. A low toss cannot be corrected (no full extension, poor contact angle), whereas a high toss can be waited for. All high-level players hit the ball on the way down. Most top servers use a high toss. A low toss with a quick rhythm suits only very explosive players. | A1 5:45–7:14; A4 0:35–4:42; A33 1:45–2:07 |
| Practice | Never practise with the toss arm alone. Always move the hitting arm too: toss to 1 o'clock and catch it at the top, 10 in a row. Toss into a bin in front of the front foot until 20 go in. Go through the whole rhythm and freeze just before contact. Spend 10–15 minutes daily. Beginners may start with dry tosses at the net. A lag rhythm "compartmentalises" the toss (§2.5). | A3 9:22–9:54; A8 0:31–3:43; A32 0:26–1:39; A41 57:04 |

### 2.3 Leg drive: "passive", not jumping

| What is taught (paraphrase) | Source (timestamp) |
|---|---|
| **Do not jump or actively drive the legs.** The feet should leave the ground by themselves. At high level the feet roll off the ground and the back foot leaves first, which a true jump cannot reproduce. | A10 0:00–1:25; A11 11:35–13:07 |
| The lift-off comes from straightening the trunk out of a "reverse C" (on the toes, knees bent, chest up and back) and from the shoulders reversing their tilt (the "cartwheel"). One cannot jump effectively from that posture, yet players leave the ground easily when serving. | A10 2:09–3:11; A11 14:23–15:55; A15 3:03–3:51 |
| The legs still matter: the knee bend and getting onto the toes make the C-shape possible. How much knee bend depends on the individual. How high a player leaves the ground is unimportant; leaving it at all helps, even for students in their 70s. | A11 15:06–15:55, 17:18–18:18; A10 8:32–9:53 |
| Why not jump: the timing is impossible (lift-off coincides with the racquet tip pointing down, within milliseconds), jump height varies with fatigue, and it makes loading the back foot impossible (below). | A10 3:34–5:04; A11 16:11–17:12 |
| The **back leg** supplies rhythm through the rocking motion (front → back → forward as the toss starts). Trying to "load the back foot" in the trophy position fails if the toss is in front, because the body leans forward. It works only if the ball is tossed behind the baseline, which causes long misses. Serving off the back leg alone is, he says, impossible to balance. | A12 0:04–4:10, 6:46–7:17, 9:00–9:17; A10 5:04–8:27 |
| He can reach about 80–90% of his top speed without the legs, but it strains the arm and shoulder. Serving from a chair was a power leak, "but not a huge one". The biggest power leak is losing the racquet's loop (waiter serve or shallow drop). | A11 8:52–10:27; A41 44:37–47:26 |
| He does not teach "pushing" with the legs. He teaches the whole body straightening, and contrasts this with another well-known coach's leg-push timing. | A41 51:14 |

Label: [Coaching interpretation] throughout. The speed figures are his own demonstrations [Vendor claim]. For comparison, restricting knee motion reduced ball speed by about 15–17% in a lab study (research 03 [S7]).

### 2.4 Double knee bend and its fix

| What is taught (paraphrase) | Source (timestamp) |
|---|---|
| The double knee bend is the most common knee-bend mistake at lower and intermediate recreational levels. Players bend to "help" the toss, stand, then bend again. The second bend is usually deeper and rushed, leaving no time to load. | A13 0:24–0:56 |
| **Fix:** bend once. Styles vary: Nadal stays bent throughout; Serena Williams was straight, then bent after the release. The easiest pattern is to toss with legs fairly straight and the racquet lagging, then start the bend as the racquet rises into the trophy position. | A13 1:00–2:09; A32 7:50–8:17; A33 3:49–4:11 |
| Also common: in a platform stance, the front knee pointing into the court. This locks the hip, sends the weight in too early, puts contact behind the head and limits the coil. Fix: front knee roughly parallel to the baseline, with a coil that matches the stance. (Knees pointing in opposite directions is rarer and worse.) | A13 2:21–4:49 |

Label: [Coaching interpretation]. The paywalled page adds only that the fault is common at lower recreational levels (research 04 [S9]).

### 2.5 Lag, reverse lag, racquet lag and racquet drop

Aracic uses "lag" in **two different senses**. The app's copy must not confuse them.

| Term | Meaning in Intuitive Tennis | Source (timestamp) |
|---|---|---|
| **Lag (rhythm)** | The toss arm goes first and the hitting arm "lags" behind, then chases the ball up. By his count 19 of the top 20 ATP career ace leaders serve this way (Roddick being the exception). It is continuous (no stop), separates the toss from the take-back, suits a pinpoint stance, and may add about 5–10% power. It is the easiest rhythm for most players. | A16 0:00–6:09; A17 8:07–13:13; A28 2:22–3:35 |
| Up-together and abbreviated | Up-together works if the unloading is timed well. If the arms rise too fast there is a pause at the trophy, which often causes a racquet-drop leak. The abbreviated rhythm suits only explosive players and needs a lower toss and a platform stance. | A17 0:32–3:47, 5:26–8:04; A16 1:15–1:50 |
| **Reverse lag** | The racquet arm goes up first and the toss comes second. He calls it "by far the worst rhythm", especially with a pinpoint stance, because it makes the unloading hard to time and invites a racquet-drop leak. Starting from the trophy position is fine as a teaching *progression*, not as the serve. | A17 13:13–15:36; A18 1:07–4:11 |
| **Racquet lag (whip)** | The racquet head whips out to the side just before contact. It is not a wrist lag and not the rhythm lag. It results from forward acceleration, the racquet loop and torso rotation, and is less visible on kick serves. Developing players should not try to create it. Long-time "strings-first" players may need to recalibrate by rehearsing the position hundreds of times, with a "chance, not a guarantee" of change. | A19 0:31–1:32, 7:17–10:05 |
| **Racquet drop** | At high level the racquet tip drops below the lower back. At recreational level he looks for the lower-back area. An open-face drop (Federer, Sampras) is **not** a waiter serve if the drop is deep. At the trophy: comfortably bent elbow, racquet slightly forward and away from the head. Directly over the head, or with the arm extended far out, gives a shallower drop. Do not consciously drop the racquet; it slows the acceleration. | A21 1:36–2:41, 4:14–4:24, 5:06–6:02, 7:04–8:45; A28 9:30–10:27 |
| **Racquet-drop leak** | The racquet slowly sinks into the drop before the body unloads, so acceleration starts from lower down (costing roughly 2–5 mph by his estimate). Causes: a pause at the trophy, or an isolated wrist movement. Fixes: lag rhythm; flexing the wrist early in the preparation; arriving at the trophy already loaded. It shows up on video when the racquet is already dropping before the knees start to straighten. | A20 0:07–7:08; A15 4:39–5:22; A41 51:14 |

### 2.6 Pronation: "fake pronation" and "pronation does not create slice"

| What is taught (paraphrase) | Source (timestamp) |
|---|---|
| **Fake pronation** is any attempt to pronate consciously. At full speed it comes too late, after contact, and does nothing. At slow speed it wrecks acceleration. | A22 0:22–1:32; A24 3:56–4:52 |
| What matters is **pronation into contact**: the racquet comes up on edge with the tip towards the back fence, then pronates into the ball. Every high-level player does it, and it results from fundamentals, the "power sources" and acceleration. Development takes years; he suggests 500 serves a week, or 250 if that is too many. | A22 2:03–3:31; A23 0:43–1:30; A28 10:27–11:21 |
| Continuing pronation after contact happens only on some serves, even for elite players (his own video review). It is not controllable. | A23 1:30–3:04, 6:07–6:31 |
| **Pronation does not create slice.** Slice comes from a more vertical racquet face moving laterally across the ball. Direction depends on the face angle at contact. For slice, think about the *finish*. | A23 0:17–0:22, 3:21–4:31, 12:14–12:24, 14:17–14:43 |
| Teaching image for children: "high-five the contact", like high-fiving a giant. | A23 1:13–1:30 |

### 2.7 The waiter serve and its fix

| What is taught (paraphrase) | Source (timestamp) |
|---|---|
| **Definition:** the wrist lays back, the racquet barely drops, and the strings are pushed at the ball with no pronation into contact. The results are little range of motion and little acceleration. | A25 0:03–1:13; A26 0:00–0:17 |
| **Not** waiter serves: an open-face racquet drop that is deep (Federer, Sampras), and a forehand grip with a normal drop (which is still wrong, for other reasons). | A25 1:28–2:59; A21 1:36–2:41 |
| **Cause:** it is every beginner's safest way to make contact, and it becomes habit without coaching. In higher-level recreational players, a conscious early tossing-arm drop can also make the drop shallow. | A26 1:58–2:23; A25 2:59–3:54; A41 49:20 |
| **Fix, beginners:** do not worry. Learn the fundamentals (especially synchronising the arms) and it usually disappears. | A26 0:17–1:07 |
| **Fix, chronic (years):** three "agonizing" progressions, 50 reps each: (1) start with the racquet tip down behind the back and the edge towards the ball, toss, and "high-five" the ball; (2) start from the "back-scratch" position (Jay Berger's serve) and whip the racquet out; (3) start from the trophy, drop, then swing up. Ignore whether the ball goes in. Do not progress until the current step is right, and film it if no coach is present. Expect the waiter serve to return at first. Allow one to two years. An alternative chronic fix: consciously drop the racquet low (or ease slowly into the drop) before accelerating, for a couple of months only. It would be a mistake on a sound serve. | A26 1:07–8:02; A25 3:57–7:15 |

### 2.8 Contact point (and the jackknife)

| What is taught (paraphrase) | Source (timestamp) |
|---|---|
| Flat and slice contact are slightly in front of the head. Kick contact is further back, above the head. The toss must match the intended contact. Contact behind the body is a "catastrophe": less power, possible shoulder strain and long misses. | A6 0:45–1:03, 1:58–2:30, 3:19–4:46; A34 0:37–0:44; A28 11:21–12:13 |
| Full extension at contact. A toss that is too low prevents it. The ball should be struck while falling. | A1 5:45–7:14; A33 1:45–2:07 |
| Players cannot perceive their own contact point; they judge by feel and result, working in zones. | A28 12:13–12:35; A6 5:24–5:45 |
| **Jackknife:** the upper body folds forward while the legs are pushed back. It is most often caused by a toss behind (the legs kick back), and also by a toss in front without a whole-body lean. **Fix:** toss inside the court on every serve, and lean the whole body so that a straight line runs from the back foot to the front shoulder. The legs then straighten and the player lands inside the court. | A27 0:00–1:12; A6 4:46–4:55 |

The paywalled "Contact Point" rec-level video (3:40) remains unseen. [Coaching interpretation]

### 2.9 The "1-2-3 rhythm" (not found) and rhythm in general

- **"1-2-3 rhythm" is a lesson title in the paid course** (research 04 [S2]). No free video, description or interview explains it. Searches of the two channels' titles and descriptions and of our 45 transcripts found no "1-2-3" serve content. Its teaching is **UNVERIFIED**, so research 04's F4 and F10 drills can no longer cite it (§3).
- What the free material does teach about rhythm: the lag rhythm is the easiest (§2.5). Go slow to fast, building speed gradually. Avoid a fast start that forces a pause at the trophy (A9 5:50–7:25; A24 0:20–0:37). The back leg's rocking motion supplies rhythm (A12 0:04–1:32). [Coaching interpretation]

### 2.10 "Control before power" and "power must come gradually"

- Both are **paid-course lesson titles** (research 04 [S2]). Their content is **UNVERIFIED**.
- Free material that is *consistent* with the titles, without being evidence of what the lessons say [Our inference]:
  - The beginner progression teaches the toss, then contact with the hand, then contact with the racquet, then *directional control* (serving diagonally into the right box), and only then the racquet loop, the lag take-back, body loading and the full rhythm. He calls teaching a beginner the full motion "the worst thing you can do" (A32 0:26–8:42).
  - Flat serve first, then slice, then kick. Tapping in a flat second serve for a while is acceptable (A33 0:00–1:02, 13:13–13:32).
  - When hitting hard, even the best servers make only about 60–70% of first serves, so do not chase perfection (A34 5:01–5:18) [Vendor claim for the figure].
  - A separate groundstroke lesson calibrates speed in "gears" and advises never using the top gear (A40 2:10–6:02). It is not about the serve.
- Note: "gradual acceleration" in the free lessons (A24 0:20–0:37) describes speed *within one motion* (slow to fast). It is not the same claim as "power must come gradually" over weeks of practice. Research 04 §4.5 conflated the two, and this is now corrected.

### 2.11 Other verified content used later

| Topic | What is taught (paraphrase) | Source (timestamp) |
|---|---|---|
| Tossing arm after release | Keep the toss arm rising after release so the front shoulder is above the hitting shoulder (tilt). The tilt reverses on its own as the racquet goes up (the "cartwheel"). **Do not consciously pull the toss arm down**: it happens too early and too fast and destroys vertical momentum. The tuck at contact happens by itself. For flat and slice, think of moving the arm *out of the way* after contact to allow rotation. On kick, stay sideways. | A15 0:48–2:20; A14 0:07–4:24 |

Forehand grips must be changed because kick and slice become impossible, although a forehand grip does *not* necessarily prevent a racquet drop. **Comparison with D1 and research 06 [Our inference].**
- D1's side view covers the Kovacs checkpoints that are about *height and timing*: release height, toss drop, the lowest hip point and its rise, contact and landing. These are also the ones D5 already measures.
- Two Stage 2/3 checkpoints that Kovacs's 80/20 view treats as root causes cannot be seen side-on: **tossing-arm direction** and **hip turn**. The same applies to hip/shoulder tilt. The optional rear view (D1) is the only way to observe them. The capture guide should therefore present the rear view as "unlocks toss direction and hip turn" as well as outcomes, and the app should never infer these from the side view. Frame rate: Kovacs's lab uses far higher rates than a phone. Nothing in his public material requires more than D1's 60 fps for the release, drop, hip-rise and landing checkpoints. Cocking and long-axis rotation remain out of scope, as research 03 and D5 already decided.
- Number of serves: Kovacs publishes none. Research 06's minimums (at least 5 serves for a mechanics observation, at least 20 per stratum for toss spread) stand.

## 6. Referring to these coaches without implying endorsement

### 6.1 The legal and platform constraints

- **Misleading conduct [Regulation].**
  - ACL s 18 prohibits conduct in trade or commerce that is misleading or deceptive, or likely to be (A76).
  - s 29(1)(g) prohibits false or misleading representations that goods or services have sponsorship or approval. s 29(1)(h) covers representations that the business itself has a sponsorship, approval or affiliation. s 29(1)(e)–(f) cover testimonials (A76).
  - The ACCC stresses that intent is irrelevant and the overall impression counts (A72).
  - Penalties for a company (s 224(3A)): the greater of **A$100,000,000**, three times the benefit, or 30% of adjusted turnover. The cap was doubled from A$50 million for contraventions from 28 March 2026 (A69, A76; A70 is a law-firm summary). For individuals the cap is A$2,500,000.
  - Recent example: the Federal Court ordered JustAnswer to pay A$10 million (8 July 2026), partly for falsely implying affiliation with the Fair Work Ombudsman (A71).
- **How that applies to us [Our inference; needs legal review].** Crediting a drill or cue to a named coach who did not teach it risks misrepresentation, and it may also infringe that coach's moral right against false attribution (A73). Using a coach's name prominently, in a way that suggests the app embodies their method, could imply approval or affiliation. The research 04 F10 "1-2-3 rhythm" attribution was exactly this risk. A disclaimer does not cure a misleading overall impression.
- **Copyright [Regulation].** Copyright protects the *expression* of ideas in forms such as writing, film and images (A73). Coaching ideas and methods described in our own words are generally usable. Transcripts, video frames, drill names used as product labels and long paraphrases that track a lesson's structure are not. The fair-dealing exceptions (research or study, criticism or review, and others; A73) do not cover shipping another coach's lesson content in a commercial app.
- **Platform terms.**
  - YouTube's Terms bar reproducing, downloading, distributing or modifying content without permission, bar automated access, and limit viewing to personal, non-commercial use (A44). So: no clips, screenshots, transcripts or re-hosted media in the app.
  - intuitivetennis.com runs on Vimeo OTT (VHX). Its terms page renders by script, and its default title is "Vimeo OTT Viewer Terms of Service Agreement". That standard agreement licenses programmes for personal viewing only and prohibits commercial use, redistribution, public display and derivative works (A42, A43). Which exact version applies to Intuitive Tennis is UNVERIFIED. Either way, **buying the course or subscription would not license its content for the app.**
  - Kovacs Academy members' content is behind a login and must not be used.
  - "8 Stage Model" comes from a peer-reviewed paper that is free to read (PMC), and it may be *cited and described*. No open licence is recorded (A75), so do not reproduce its figures or text.
- **Names and trademarks.** "Intuitive Tennis", "Tennis Serve Specialist", "Serve Screen", "Ki Serve Loading Drill" and "Ki Shot Put Serve Drill" function as brand or product names. Their registration status was not checked (UNVERIFIED; check IP Australia before launch). Do not use them, or "intuitive zone", as feature, drill or plan names.

So no coach names in App Store keywords, subtitles or screenshots.

1. **Cues and drills are written in our own words and carry the D2 evidence tier ("research-backed", "coach-recommended", "experimental"), never a person's name.** No "Aracic's drill", "the Kovacs cue" or "as taught by".

## Implications for the product

1. **Apply the 04 corrections before any cue copy is written** (§3). This matters most for F4, F10, F12, F14 and F17, where the old wording either contradicts both named experts or misattributes to them.
2. **Legs:** use the "short crouch, quick rise" and "meet the ball at your highest reach" defaults. A back-leg-specific cue or drill is always labelled "coaches differ".
3. **Toss:** keep toss consistency as the hero metric (D5). Add an *experimental* "release height relative to the eyes" metric (a Kovacs checkpoint that both schools share). Do not prescribe a toss height.
4. **Candidate experimental metric:** a "leg-drive speed band" (back-hip vertical velocity from the lowest point to take-off, side view, 60 fps), to be validated against the synthetic generator and annotated clips before any in-app use.
5. **Capture guide copy:** the rear view "unlocks toss direction and hip turn" as well as outcomes.
6. **Never cue:** pronation, wrist, conscious racquet drop, pulling the tossing arm down, the front hip, or the landing as a primary cue.
7. **Legal:** build the attribution register and source sheet into the content pipeline; no coach names in any marketing or metadata.
