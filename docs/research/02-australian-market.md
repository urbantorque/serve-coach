# 02 — Australian tennis market and candidate customer segments

*Research date: 27 September 2026. Scope: who in Australia would use and pay for a phone-based serve coach aimed at recreational players (roughly UTR 1–7). Written in Australian English. All prices are in AUD (the original currency) unless stated otherwise.*

**Evidence labels used:** [Established] peer-reviewed or strong consensus; [Coaching interpretation] expert practice, not directly proven; [Vendor claim] a company says so, unverified; [Market data] participation, pricing, competition bylaws and other industry facts; [Regulation] law or platform rules only; [Our inference] our own reasoning. **UNVERIFIED** marks anything we could not confirm from a primary page.

**Method notes and limits.**
- Many tennis.com.au pages returned HTTP 403 or 404 to our fetcher. For a few Tennis Australia (TA) facts we therefore rely on search-result excerpts, and we say so where it applies.
- We could not open the TA Annual Report 2024–25 (15.7 MB). Its CEO summary is reprinted in the Tennis ACT Annual Report 2024–25, which we did read [S8].
- The detailed AusPlay tennis dashboard (age, frequency, club versus non-club for 2025) is Power BI only and could not be fetched [S2][S51]. Age splits below therefore come from the 2021–22 AusPlay national report [S4].
- Our web-search budget ran out part-way through. Regional-town coaching prices and some state competition totals remain gaps (see Open questions).

## 1. Executive summary

- **Size.** About **1.12 million Australians aged 15+** played tennis at least once in 2025: 648,000 men and 469,000 women. Another **187,000 children aged 0–14** also played [S1] [Market data]. Tennis was among the fastest-growing activities in the 2024–25 AusPlay year-on-year comparison [S3] [Market data].
- **Most adult tennis is unstructured.**
  - The most popular adult activities are non-organised: 66% of active adults take part in activities they organise themselves, and 50% with friends (AusPlay, July 2023–June 2024) [S9] [Market data].
  - TA's FY2023–24 report counts 122.6k players at its affiliated clubs and venues, including 24.3k club members and 103.2k casual players, so casual players outnumber club members about 4:1 [S7] [Market data]. The report does not define the scope of this count, and the two categories add up to more than the total, so they overlap (fact-check note).
  - Online court hire passed **1 million hours in about 12 months** to October 2024 [S36].
  - SportLogic recorded **2.37 million hours** booked in 2024, up 13% [S37] [Market data].
  - This is the solo-practice infrastructure a serve coach needs: bookable courts, often with PIN-gate and light access [S40].
- **Ratings are part of competitive club tennis.**
  - TA replaced its National Rating System with UTR in 2022 [S18].
  - Competitive Player Profiles (a Tennis ID linked to a UTR) are required for some pennant play [S16].
  - More than **100,000 rated players** now compete regularly in leagues and tournaments [S8].
  - Victorian Pennant uses UTR-based playing order [S14] [Market data].
  - So a "UTR 1–7 player" is a real, identifiable, reachable person in Australia.
- **Coaching is expensive and scarce outside the cities.**
  - A 30-minute private lesson in the capital cities (plus the Gold Coast) we sampled costs **$50–$85**; 60 minutes costs **$80–$140** [S21–S33] [Market data]. The $50 and $80 floors are Gold Coast rates; casual capital-city rates start at about $52 (30 min) and $90 (60 min) [S27].
  - TA has a record **3,766 coach members**, roughly one for every ~350 participants [S8] [Our inference].
  - Regional towns have gone a year or more without a coach [S34], and a Brisbane operator had paused private lessons because of increased demand and staff shortages [S27] [Market data].
- **Pickleball and padel compete for the same adults and courts.**
  - Padel: 90,000+ players, up 50% year on year [S11].
  - Pickleball Australia: 27,974 registered players across 425 clubs (June 2026) [S12].
  - TA estimates 170,000 participants in "complementary disciplines" at 250+ tennis venues [S8] [Market data].
- **Ranking, not a final pick.** Seven candidate segments are ranked in §9. The strongest early combination is **adult competition players (pennant or night comp, UTR ~3–7) who practise on bookable courts**, reached partly through their **coaches**. Regional players and returning adults come next. Juniors and masters are large but carry extra product and regulatory risk [Our inference].
  - *Gap-fill update (2026-09-27): a primary segment has now been picked and sized in `gaps/australian-segment-evidence.md`. It is rated adult competition players at UTR ~3–7, about 35,000–60,000 nationally, with a metropolitan Melbourne beachhead. The secondary segment is coach-led UTR ~1–3 improvers.*

- AusPlay changed its method in July 2023 and doubled its sample to about 40,000 people. The ASC warns that 2023+ results cannot be compared directly with 2015–2023 results [S2][S3][S51] [Market data].

- **Location.** In 2021–22, about **919,000** adult tennis players lived in major cities (544k men, 375k women) and about **261,000** in regional or rural areas (140k men, 121k women). That puts roughly **21–22% of adult players outside the major cities** [S4] [Market data; share is Our inference].

- **Age.** Older Roy Morgan data (2015–16) shows participation peaking in the teens (16.2% of 14–17-year-olds) and falling to 4.3% at 50–64 and 2.9% at 65+ [S10] [Market data; likely outdated].

Roy Morgan also found above-average participation among people born in China, India and South Africa [S10] [Market data]. Localised onboarding may matter later [Our inference].

### 2.3 Pickleball and padel: competitors for time and courts

- **Pickleball.**
  - AusPlay recorded more than 92,000 Australians who had played in the year to June 2024 [S12] [Market data].
  - Pickleball Australia members grew from 15,166 (2023–24) to 22,209 (2024–25), with 27,974 registered players across 425 clubs by 29 June 2026 [S12] [Market data; Wikipedia summarising Pickleball Australia; the association's own site failed our TLS check].
- **Padel.**
  - 90,000+ Australians have played (+50% year on year).
  - There are 100+ courts (+40%) and 10 new clubs in 12 months (December 2025) [S11] [Market data / sport-body claim]. Padel Australia does not state the data source behind the 90,000 figure.
- **Tennis venues are absorbing both sports.**
  - FY2023–24: investor and operator enquiries about new disciplines rose more than 300%, and 210 venues programmed padel, POP or pickleball [S7].
  - FY2024–25: about 170,000 participants at 250+ venues [S8] [Market data].
  - Wangaratta Lawn Tennis Club (regional Victoria) now has four pickleball courts beside eight tennis courts [S40] [Market data].
- **Implications.**
  - Pickleball in particular recruits the same 45+ adults as social tennis and competes for court time [Our inference].
  - Tennis serves are a tennis-only problem, so these sports are not a threat to the product's core job. They are a threat to the size of the tennis-playing adult pool, and a reason to position tennis as "a sport where you can keep improving" [Our inference].

## 4. How UTR is used in Australia

- **Partnership history.**
  - TA and Universal Tennis signed a strategic partnership on 19–20 January 2019, recognising UTR as an official rating [S19] [Market data].
  - In January 2022 TA became the first national governing body to adopt UTR in place of its own National Rating System, across junior events, team competitions and leagues [S18] [Market data].
- **Data flow.**
  - UTR imports TA results weekly from TA's tournament database: Junior Tour, money tournaments, Seniors, JDS, national opens and Club Championships.
  - A result counts towards a verified UTR if the opponent is within 2.00 UTR, within the player's 30 most recent results over 12 months [S17] [Vendor claim].
  - Players link their TA Tennis ID to UTR through single sign-on. There is a specific pathway for Australians under 13 [S17b] [Vendor claim].
- **Scale.**
  - In FY2023–24, 80,000+ players had Competitive Player Profiles [S7].
  - In FY2024–25, 100,000+ rated players were "regularly participating" [S8] [Market data].
  - This group includes juniors; we found no public adult-only figure (**UNVERIFIED**).
- **UTR distribution of Australian adult club players.**
  - **Not publicly available** (UNVERIFIED). Neither TA nor UTR publishes it.
  - TV's grading guide maps grades to UTR bands but could not be fetched (403).
  - We recommend asking TA or UTR directly, or collecting UTR in onboarding (see Open questions).
  - *(Gap-fill note, 2026-09-27: a search excerpt of TV's 2023 Pennant info sheet gives men's team-average UTR thresholds of 9.54 (Grade 3), 8.79 (Grade 4) and 8.24 (Grade 5). These are **UNVERIFIED** (PDF 403). If correct, TV Pennant's upper and middle men's grades sit above UTR 7. UTR 1–2 adults are largely outside graded competition. See `gaps/australian-segment-evidence.md` §3.)*
  - Third-party UTR↔NTRP conversions exist but are not authoritative, so we do not use them.
- **Product implication.**
  - Treat UTR as a **player-entered field** (label: player-entered) for segmentation and progress context.
  - Do not assume API access to UTR or TA data without a partnership [Our inference].

## 5. Coaching access and prices

### 5.1 Coach supply

- **Numbers.**
  - TA had a record **3,766 coach members** in FY2024–25, including 1,200+ women [S8] [Market data].
  - In FY2023–24, coach membership rose 17% and trainee memberships rose 45%; women were 32% of coaches [S7] [Market data].
  - In 2022, TA cited about 3,000 accredited coaches nationally, roughly one-third of them in Victoria [S34] [Market data].
- **Ratio.** About 1.3 million participants against 3,766 coach members is roughly **one coach per ~350 players** [Our inference]. Many coaches work part-time or mainly with juniors (**UNVERIFIED**).
- **Few members hold senior qualifications.** In the ACT, 129 of 190 coach members held the Trainee *membership category*, with 33 Qualified and 27 Business coach members [S8] [Market data]. The membership category is not the same as qualification level. By qualification, 147 (77%) held a formal coaching qualification, but only 36 held Club Professional or higher (11 High Performance, 1 Master Club Professional, 24 Club Professional) [S8] [Market data]. The pool of experienced coaches who can diagnose adult serve mechanics is therefore much smaller than the headline figure suggests [Our inference].
- **Regional shortages.**
  - In 2022, Swan Hill (Victoria) had been without its only coach for more than a year, and Ararat, Orbost, Kerang and Mallacoota had no coach at all.
  - Causes cited: rental shortages, seasonality, and competition from city jobs [S34] [Market data].
  - In September 2025 Tennis NSW announced a "Regional Coach Activation & Retention Program" [S35]. The title and date come from a search result, and the URL returned 404 at fact-check, so the program and its details are **UNVERIFIED**. TA's Cardio Tennis volunteer pilots target regional areas, which TA says hold about 7 million adults [S7] [Market data].
- **Metro shortages too.** In September 2026 a Brisbane operator's private-lesson page stated that no private lessons were available at three centres because of increased demand and staff shortages [S27] [Market data].

**Takeaways.**
- **Price of feedback.** Across the capital cities and the Gold Coast, a 30-minute private lesson (the smallest unit of one-to-one feedback) costs **$50–$85**, and an hour costs **$80–$140** [Market data].
  - Weekly 30-minute lessons for 40 weeks cost about **$2,000–$3,400 a year** (40 × $50 to 40 × $85) [Our inference].
  - An app priced at roughly one lesson per quarter is a small fraction of that [Our inference].
- **Group formats are cheap ($15–$43 a session)** but give little individual serve feedback. Group coaching typically spends little time on each player's serve mechanics [Coaching interpretation; UNVERIFIED as a measured fact]. This leaves a gap between cheap group play and expensive privates, which a serve coach could fill [Our inference].
- **Lessons are sold as packs.** Pack discounts are about 5–12% [S22][S24][S32]. A "lesson + app homework" bundle sold through coaches is commercially natural [Our inference].

## 6. Court access: booking, gates, lights and surfaces

### 6.1 Booking and gate access

- **Book a Court.**
  - TA's Book a Court runs on ClubSpark and began as a Victorian pilot in 2015–16. It combines online booking and payment with automated gate and light control. By 2017 it covered 56 venues and 486 courts, with 53,000+ bookings [S38] [Vendor claim].
  - A TA page states there are more than 3,000 bookable courts (search excerpt; page 403, **UNVERIFIED**) [S39].
- **Growth in bookings.**
  - FY2023–24: 900+ of 2,329 affiliated venues had online booking, online bookings rose 25%, and 220+ venues offered "alternative gate access" [S7] [Market data].
  - 1 million hours booked in about 12 months to October 2024 [S36][S8] [Market data].
  - SportLogic data: 2,372,042 hours in 2024, up 13% [S37] [Market data].
  - In the ACT (FY2024–25): 33 venues, 55,549 bookings (+7%), 77,791 hours, and a **39% rise in unique players booking courts** [S8] [Market data].
- **How access works: a regional example.**
  - At Wangaratta Lawn Tennis Club, members book free (lights extra) and visitors pay from $20 an hour.
  - A 4-digit PIN code sent in the booking confirmation email opens the gate and switches on the lights. It works from 10 minutes before to 15 minutes after the booking [S40] [Market data].
  - This is the "record a few serves alone at a local court" scenario in its purest form [Our inference].
- **Metro prices.**
  - Tennis World Melbourne: $35–$46/hr outdoors and $53–$64/hr indoors. Casual non-member bookings open only 24 hours ahead [S26] [Market data].
  - Rushcutters Bay (Sydney): $32/hr [S23] [Market data].
- **Ownership.** Local government owns most Australian tennis infrastructure [S41] [Market data]. Council courts are the dominant setting for solo practice outside clubs [Our inference].

### 6.2 Surfaces, colours, lighting and orientation (inputs to capture guidance)

- **Recognised surfaces.**
  - TA's surface policy prefers Grand Slam surfaces (acrylic hard, natural clay, natural grass).
  - It also supports loam, ant-bed and en-tout-cas clays, synthetic clay and sand-filled artificial grass (SFAG) for community participation [S41] [Market data].
  - A court-engineering firm describes SFAG as "one of the most common" community surfaces [S42] [Vendor claim]. We found no national count of courts by surface (**UNVERIFIED**).
- **Natural grass is still in use.**
  - TA says natural grass "remains popular" in some states [S41].
  - WA weekday women's 45+/60+ pennant doubles are played on grass [S16] [Market data].
- **Colours.** We found no authoritative national breakdown (**UNVERIFIED**). From provider imagery and TA guidance [S41][S42] [Our inference]:
  - Australian community courts come in blue or green acrylic, green or terracotta SFAG (sometimes blue), red or brown clay and en-tout-cas, and natural green grass.
  - Lines on SFAG are often sand-covered and less crisp than on acrylic. This matters for court-line detection and calibration [Our inference].
- **Lighting.**
  - TA planning guidance gives **250 lux for recreational tennis and 350 lux for competition**, and stresses uniformity [S41] [Market data].
  - LED upgrades are spreading; 31 ACT venues have LED lighting [S8] [Market data].
  - Phone footage at high frame rates (120–240 fps) under 250–350 lux is likely to be noisy or motion-blurred. Capture guidance should prefer daylight for high-fps work and warn about night sessions [Our inference; to be tested].
- **Orientation.** TA recommends a north–south court axis (within 15° of true north) to limit sun in players' eyes [S41] [Market data]. Another section of the same guide gives wider ideal limits (20° west to 35° east of north), so real courts vary. Early-morning and late-afternoon sessions therefore produce strong side-lighting and long shadows across the court. Setup guidance should mention this [Our inference].

## 7. Adult learner and social programs

- **Cardio Tennis.**
  - In FY2023–24 TA relaunched it with four streams (Classic, Lite, Play, Max); the delivery network grew 9% [S7] [Market data].
  - Typical price: $20–$32.50 a session [S29][S30][S33] [Market data].
  - Cardio Tennis prioritises fitness over technique, so participants who want a better serve are a natural upgrade audience [Our inference].
- **Adult group coaching.**
  - Terms are 8–10 weeks, levelled from beginner to advanced [S23][S28][S30] [Market data].
  - A beginner-to-intermediate adult who "can serve and rally" is the entry point for supervised match play [S30] [Market data].
- **TA strategy.**
  - TA's strategic objectives include to "get adults back into tennis" through promotion of how, where and with whom to play [S7] [Market data].
  - Volunteer-delivered Hot Shots and Cardio Tennis are expanding into more communities [S8] [Market data].
- **TA's own innovation pipeline.**
  - AO StartUps pilots external technology inside TA and the AO. The 2024 cohort included motion and movement-analysis companies.
  - AO Labs built an internal "Coaches Box" analytics tool [S7] [Market data].
  - These are a possible partnership channel, and TA may also become a competitor [Our inference].

## 9. Candidate segments: evidence and ranking

**How to read this section.**
- Scores run from 1 (weak) to 5 (strong). "Solo practice" means how often the segment is likely to be alone on a court with time to serve.
- "Capture fit" means how well a one-phone, one-angle setup suits the segment.
- "Risk" is scored inversely: 5 means low regulatory or product risk.
- The scores are our judgement [Our inference], based on the cited evidence.

### 9.1 Segment profiles

**A. Adult competition players (pennant or night comp), UTR ~3–7, ages ~25–60, practising on bookable courts**
- **Size.**
  - 100,000+ rated players compete regularly, including juniors [S8].
  - Named comps include VIC Pennant (419 teams / 135 clubs) [S13], ACT CTL (~1,342–1,400 players, adults and juniors combined; 499 adult teams) [S8], and the WA and SA pennants [S15][S16].
  - A national adult comp population in the **tens of thousands** is plausible; the exact figure is **UNVERIFIED**.
- **Pain.**
  - Serve outcomes are public and cost matches [Our inference].
  - UTR playing order and grading make standard visible [S14][S15].
  - Private lessons cost $100–$140 an hour [S21–S25].
- **Solo practice.**
  - Serves are the one stroke you can practise without a partner [Coaching interpretation].
  - Courts can be booked by the hour with PIN access [S40].
- **Spending.** This group already pays club fees, team fees ($115 per team in SA [S15], **UNVERIFIED**) and lessons [Market data].
- **Reachability.** High: club captains, pennant ecosystems, UTR and Tennis ID events, Facebook groups, coaches.
- **Measurable outcomes.** First-serve % and double faults can be player-entered from matches and compared with session statistics. This links directly to the brief's "Am I getting more first serves in?" [Our inference].

**B. Coaches who want between-lesson homework and serve evidence (B2B2C channel)**
- **Size.** 3,766 TA coach members [S8], each with dozens of students [Our inference].
- **Pain.**
  - Too few coaches, especially regionally [S34][S27].
  - Lesson time is expensive for students.
  - Coaches cannot see students' practice [Our inference].
- **Solo practice.** Indirect: coaches assign it.
- **Spending.** Coaches pay for business tools, but many are trainees or part-time [S8]. Willingness to pay is moderate, which favours "free for coach, paid by student" models [Our inference].
- **Reachability.** Very high through TA coach membership communications, coach exchanges (1,278 in FY2023–24 [S7]), coach summits and state coach-development staff [Market data].
- **Why it matters.** Coach endorsement lends credibility to the coaching cues [Our inference]. It also reduces the risk of a "the app contradicts my coach" conflict [Our inference].

**C. Returning or improving adults, 30–55, who are casual court bookers (non-club)**
- **Size.**
  - 437,000 adult players aged 35–54 in 2021–22 [S4].
  - Casual players outnumber club members about 4:1 at reporting venues [S7].
  - Most adult activity is self-organised: 66% of active adults take part in activities they organise themselves [S9].
- **Pain.**
  - No structured feedback.
  - Group classes are cheap but generic; privates are costly.
  - Many learned to serve as children and want to "fix" it [Our inference].
- **Solo practice.** Medium to high: this group posts in hitting-partner groups (17.4K / 13.6K / 5.2K members) [S43–S45] and books courts online [S36].
- **Spending.** Moderate, and more price-sensitive than comp players [Our inference].
- **Reachability.** Medium. They are diffuse, but reachable through Book a Court venues, Facebook groups, Cardio Tennis and adult group programs.

**D. Regional and rural players far from coaches**
- **Size.** About 261,000 adult players (~21–22%) lived outside major cities in 2021–22 [S4]. TA cites about 7 million adults in regional areas as a target market [S7].
- **Pain.** Highest of all segments. Some towns have no coach at all [S34], and state bodies run coach-retention grants [S35].
- **Solo practice.** High. Courts are cheap or free with PIN access [S40], but finding partners is harder [Our inference].
- **Spending.** Likely lower average income [S9] (**UNVERIFIED** for tennis), but also fewer substitutes, so willingness to pay may be similar [Our inference].
- **Reachability.** Medium to low: dispersed, but reachable through regional associations, country championships and state bodies' regional programs.
- **Product needs.** Patchy mobile coverage at country courts would favour on-device processing and deferred upload (**UNVERIFIED**; to test) [Our inference].

**E. Masters / veterans, 50–70, protecting their shoulders**
- **Size.**
  - 338,000 adult players aged 55+ (2021–22) [S4].
  - Organised 30+/45+/60+ comps and seniors associations exist [S16][S49][S50].
- **Pain.**
  - One study looked at 113 high-level senior players (aged 35+, competing at the French national championships; mean age 57; 64.6% female). 76.1% reported no current shoulder pain, yet **78.8% of all players showed structural shoulder abnormalities** and 46.0% had rotator-cuff tears. The dominant shoulder had much higher odds of supraspinatus tears (OR 8.7, 95% CI 3.1–24.3) [S46] [Established: a single peer-reviewed cross-sectional study; applying it to recreational players is Our inference]. *(Fact-check correction: 78.8% is the share of all 113 players, not of pain-free shoulders.)*
  - Former elite players showed 33% dominant-shoulder osteoarthritis against 11% in controls (n=18 per group, p = 0.04). Changes were mostly minimal (5 of 6 cases) [S47] [Established; small sample].
- **Solo practice.** Medium. Many play midweek doubles [S16], and serve practice volume may be limited by comfort [Our inference].
- **Spending.** High disposable income and time [Our inference; UNVERIFIED].
- **Reachability.** Medium. Seniors associations and midweek club comps are identifiable, but app adoption may be lower (**UNVERIFIED**).
- **Risk.** The product must not diagnose. It needs careful copy around "comfort" and "see a professional" (brief requirement) [Our inference].

**F. Parents filming junior players**
- **Size.** 187,000 children played in 2025 [S1]; junior comp entries rose 16% [S7].
- **Pain.** Parents already pay for privates ($110–$120/hr for kids at one Sydney venue [S22]) and want between-lesson progress [Our inference].
- **Solo practice.** Medium, with the parent as camera operator [Our inference].
- **Spending.** High: junior tennis is costlier than many team sports [S6] [Market data, secondary].
- **Reachability.** High through junior tournaments, JDS and coaches [S7].
- **Risk.**
  - Processing children's video raises heightened privacy obligations under Australian law and Apple's rules for apps involving minors (to be confirmed in the privacy research doc; **UNVERIFIED here**).
  - Coaches may resist parent-led technical advice [Our inference].
  - Junior biomechanics are still developing, which calls for different norms [Coaching interpretation].

**G. FIFO and shift workers**
- **Size.** More than 50,000 FIFO workers in WA alone, per a 2012 federal inquiry [S48] [Market data; **outdated**]. The share who play tennis is unknown (**UNVERIFIED**).
- **Pain.** Rosters prevent weekly lessons and fixed-night comps [Our inference].
- **Solo practice.** High on rostered days off, using lit PIN-access courts [Our inference].
- **Spending.** High incomes [Our inference; UNVERIFIED].
- **Reachability.** Low. There is no identifiable tennis channel.

## Implications for the product

In priority order:

1. **Design v1 for adult competition players (UTR ~3–7).**
   - Frame the statistics around match questions they already ask: "first serves in", "double faults", "toss consistency".
   - Add a player-entered match log for first-serve % and double faults, labelled *player-entered*, so session statistics can be compared with match outcomes [S14][S15][S16].
2. **Ship a coach-share path in the first release.**
   - Let a player share a session, key frames and chosen cue with their coach, free for the coach.
   - This addresses the coach shortage [S27][S34] and turns coaches into an acquisition channel with 3,766 potential advocates [S8].
3. **Make capture guidance specific to Australian courts.**
   - Cover blue and green acrylic, green or terracotta SFAG with sandy lines, natural grass (WA) and clay or en-tout-cas.
   - Account for north–south orientation (low side sun early and late) and 250–350 lux night lighting [S41][S16].
   - Recommend daylight for high-frame-rate capture, and test detection and calibration on SFAG lines specifically.
4. **Assume solo sessions on bookable courts.**
   - Include a "booked court, 30–60 minutes" session template, e.g. warm-up, 20–30 recorded serves, review, drill.
   - Size the flow to a $20–$46 hourly court booking [S23][S26][S40].
5. **Price against lessons and group classes, not other apps.**
   - A 30-minute private costs $50–$85 and a group class $15–$43 [S21–S33].
   - A paid tier costing less than one private lesson a month is easy to justify. The monetisation workstream should test price points against these anchors.
6. **Plan launch timing around Australian seasons.**
   - Target a beta before winter pennant (February–March) or before summer comps and the AO (September–October).
   - Use each season as a natural retention and before/after measurement window [S16].
7. **Test offline-first processing for regional users.** This matters if connectivity at country courts proves poor; the evidence is still unverified.
8. **Treat UTR as optional and player-entered.** Do not depend on UTR or TA data access until a partnership exists [S17][S18].
9. **Defer the junior and parent segment** until child-privacy and consent design is complete. **Defer masters-specific features** until pain and comfort copy has been reviewed by a clinician.
10. **Use hitting-partner communities (17.4K / 13.6K / 5.2K) and club captains for early recruitment** [S43–S45]. Explore TA's AO StartUps program as a partnership route, and keep in mind that TA could also build a competing tool [S7].
11. **Validation metrics by segment.**
    - A: 4-week retention through a comp season; share of users logging match serve stats; change in self-reported first-serve %.
    - B: coach-share rate and coach-assigned drills per student.
    - C and D: second-session rate within 14 days.
    - All segments: trial-to-paid conversion at price points anchored to lesson costs.
